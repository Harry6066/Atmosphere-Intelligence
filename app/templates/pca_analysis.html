Mathematical Foundations: Principal Component Analysis

Hotelling (1933) / Pearson (1901)
1 Mandatory Standard Scaling

Weather observations span fundamentally disparate physical units: temperatures in °C, precipitation in tenths of mm, snow depth in mm, and cyclic DOY in [-1, +1]. Without StandardScaler(z = (x - μ) / σ), high-variance variables like precipitation would falsely dominate the covariance structure.

2 Covariance Eigendecomposition

PCA computes the sample covariance matrix Σ = (1/N) X^T X and solves the characteristic equation Σ v_k = λ_k v_k. The orthonormal eigenvectors v_k define the principal axes, while eigenvalues λ_k denote variance explained.

3 Orthogonal Coordinate Projection

Raw points are projected onto the eigenspace via Z = X V. The resulting components are strictly uncorrelated (Cov(PC_i, PC_j) = 0 for i ≠ j). Components 1 through 3 concentrate 56.25% of the total multivariate variability.

Scientific Limitations: Interpreting Low-Dimensional Weather Projections

Methodological Integrity
Residual Variance Loss (43.75%)

While 2D and 3D projections capture 44.05% and 56.25% of variance respectively, 43.75% of multidimensional information remains in components 4 through 10. Consequently, two observations that appear close together in 2D space may be sharply separated in higher dimensions.

Linear Projection vs Nonlinear Manifolds

PCA enforces linear orthogonal transformations. Weather dynamics often reside on complex nonlinear manifolds (e.g., precipitation thresholds, freezing phase changes). An anomaly may not visually isolate along linear eigenvectors even though it represents a physical anomaly.

Visualization Tool, Not Detector

PCA is employed here as a diagnostic lens to inspect cluster geometry, not as the primary anomaly detection algorithm. True anomaly flags are assigned by Isolation Forest (orthogonal cuts) and LOF (density ratios) in the full 10-dimensional scaled space.
